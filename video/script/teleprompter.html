Recording the voice for the Steady video

  • What to record: sound only. Voice Memos on a phone is fine, or QuickTime, File, New Audio Recording.
  • Where: a quiet room with soft things in it (a bedroom beats a kitchen). Hold the phone about a hand's length from your mouth.
  • How: one recording, start to finish. Press the right arrow or space for the next card and pause two seconds between cards.
  • Messed up a line? Pause, then say that whole card again from its first word and carry on. The last clean take of each card is the one that gets used.
  • Pace: calm and clear, a little slower than talking to a friend. The 326 words should take about 1 minute 45 seconds. The limit is 2 minutes.
  • Two voices? You can take turns card by card. Keep it one file, or one file each, in card order.
  • Then: put the file in video/takes/ and run python3 tools/ingest_audio.py takes/your-file.m4a, then render again. The pictures re-time themselves to your voice.

So we built Steady. It lives on a teenager's phone, linked to a parent's. Meet Maya.

3 / 10It learns her normal

For four weeks, Steady learns what's normal for her: her sleep, how often she reaches out, how much she moves, and the tone of what she types. Once a week, she checks in with two taps.

On screen: Maya's phone in the centre. The ring fills over four weeks. The four signals light up as they are named. Then she taps the two-dot check-in.
4 / 10It asks

Every month, Steady asks two questions, the ones doctors use. Maya says she's mostly okay, and Steady takes her at her word. Then finals hit. Her check-ins get tense, and our model doesn't wait a month. It asks again.

On screen: Week four: the two questions come up and she answers low. Weeks five and six: the ring turns amber, her check-ins climb, the model's score crosses its line, and the questions come up again.
5 / 10The note

She still says she's fine. But at week eight, her answers come back high. Maya sees a nudge to see a doctor, and her mom gets a note: what changed, for how long, and how to start the conversation. Never her messages.

On screen: Week six she answers low again. Week eight her answers are high. Mom's phone slides in. An amber dot crosses from Maya's phone to hers and the note opens.
6 / 10Her words stay on her phone

What Maya types never leaves her phone. A small model reads each message there, keeps one dot, and lets the words go.

On screen: Maya types a message. It becomes a bubble, then a single dot. A counter holds at 0 bytes sent.
7 / 10Does it work: the tone model

Does it work? On five thousand comments it had never seen, our tone model caught half of the low-mood ones. A keyword list caught a quarter.

On screen: Two bars grow: tone model 50%, keyword list 24%.
8 / 10Does it work: when to ask

Then we tested timing on phone data from two hundred thirteen students, and our first idea failed. Phone signals picked the week no better than chance. So we rebuilt Steady to ask first. Its model put forty-four percent of its asks on a hard week. A calendar: thirteen. And on a hundred thirteen teenagers it had never seen, it still held up.

On screen: 213 dots. 'Our first idea failed.' Then two bars: the ask model 44%, a fixed calendar 13%. Then: 113 teens, never seen, AUC 0.85.
9 / 10What it can't do yet

It knows who to ask, not yet which week. Our self-harm model can't tell someone in crisis from a friend helping them. So on its own, it only asks, and it needs a clinician.

On screen: Three short lines with icons: who, not yet which week; a crisis or a friend helping; a clinician.
